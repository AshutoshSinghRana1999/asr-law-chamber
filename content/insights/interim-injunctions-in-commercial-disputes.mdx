## Introduction

Commercial litigation often involves circumstances in which waiting for a final judgment may substantially diminish the value of the eventual relief. A party may threaten to dispose of disputed assets, disclose confidential information, encash an instrument, terminate a commercially significant arrangement or continue conduct alleged to infringe contractual or proprietary rights.

An interim injunction enables the court to preserve the subject matter of the dispute or restrain specified conduct until the parties’ rights can be finally determined. Because such an order may affect business operations before a full trial, courts ordinarily require the applicant to establish a strong factual and legal basis for immediate intervention.

An effective injunction strategy therefore depends upon urgency, accurate pleadings, reliable evidence and relief framed no wider than necessary to protect the applicant’s legitimate interests.

## Key Considerations

Before seeking an injunction, the applicant should identify the precise right requiring protection and the immediate harm likely to arise without court intervention. A general allegation that the defendant has acted unlawfully is ordinarily insufficient. The proposed injunction should correspond to an identifiable contractual, statutory, proprietary or equitable right.

The initial assessment should consider:

* the nature of the underlying commercial dispute;
* the contractual provisions governing the parties;
* the conduct sought to be restrained;
* the immediacy and seriousness of the threatened harm;
* the availability of monetary compensation;
* the effect of an injunction on the defendant and third parties;
* the evidence presently available;
* delay or acquiescence attributable to the applicant; and
* whether the proposed order can be effectively supervised and enforced.

The applicant should also determine the correct forum. Jurisdiction may depend upon the place where the cause of action arose, the location of the defendant, the subject matter of the dispute, an exclusive jurisdiction clause or an arbitration agreement.

Where the dispute qualifies as a commercial dispute of specified value under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the special procedural framework applicable to commercial suits must be followed. The pleadings, disclosure requirements and timelines in commercial litigation are generally more structured than those applicable to ordinary civil suits.

## Pre-Institution Mediation and Urgent Relief

Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act requires a plaintiff to exhaust pre-institution mediation before filing a commercial suit that does not contemplate urgent interim relief. The requirement is mandatory in suits to which the provision applies.

A plaintiff genuinely requiring urgent protection may institute the suit without first completing pre-institution mediation. However, merely inserting a prayer for interim relief does not automatically establish urgency.

The commercial court may examine the plaint, the supporting documents, the nature of the dispute and the relief claimed to determine whether urgent interim intervention is genuinely contemplated. The claim of urgency should not operate as a device to bypass the statutory mediation requirement.

The plaint should therefore explain:

* when the threat or breach arose;
* when the applicant became aware of it;
* what immediate steps were taken;
* why mediation cannot be completed without causing prejudice;
* what specific event is expected to occur; and
* why later monetary or final relief would be inadequate.

Where the applicant has known about the disputed conduct for a substantial period but has taken no action, the court may closely scrutinise the assertion of urgency. Delay does not necessarily defeat every injunction claim, particularly where the alleged breach is continuing, but it may weaken the case for immediate ex parte intervention.

Where no genuine urgency exists, the plaintiff should complete the prescribed mediation process before instituting the suit. A failure to comply with Section 12A in a case where the provision applies may result in rejection of the plaint.

## Prima Facie Case

The first traditional requirement for a temporary injunction is the existence of a prima facie case. This does not require the applicant to prove the entire suit at the interim stage. The applicant must, however, demonstrate a serious and bona fide question requiring adjudication and a credible legal right capable of protection.

The court’s assessment is provisional. It does not finally determine disputed facts or conclusively interpret the parties’ rights. Nevertheless, an injunction will not ordinarily be granted merely because the claim is arguable in an abstract sense.

In a contractual dispute, the applicant should identify:

* the relevant contractual obligation;
* the manner in which it has been or is likely to be breached;
* its own compliance with material obligations;
* any termination, notice or cure provisions;
* contractual limitations on relief; and
* the continuing enforceability of the provision relied upon.

Where the claim concerns confidential information or trade secrets, the applicant should identify the information with sufficient precision, explain why it is confidential and demonstrate the circumstances imposing an obligation of confidence.

Where the dispute concerns ownership or possession of property, the applicant should produce the title documents, possession records, correspondence and other materials supporting its asserted right.

A court is generally reluctant to grant an injunction based upon incomplete documents, unexplained contractual provisions or allegations that cannot be tested against contemporaneous records. The application should therefore be supported by the strongest evidence presently available rather than relying upon the expectation that additional evidence will emerge later.

## Balance of Convenience

The balance of convenience requires the court to compare the likely prejudice to each party. The court considers whether greater injustice would result from granting the injunction or from refusing it.

The applicant should demonstrate that the inconvenience caused by maintaining or restoring a temporary position is less serious than the harm likely to arise if the defendant is permitted to continue the disputed conduct.

Relevant considerations may include:

* disruption to an ongoing business;
* loss of customers or market position;
* risk of asset dissipation;
* interruption of essential supplies or services;
* effects on employees or third parties;
* the duration of the proposed restraint;
* whether the defendant can continue legitimate activities; and
* whether narrower protective measures are available.

An injunction should ordinarily be proportionate to the interest requiring protection. A court may refuse a broadly framed order that would effectively shut down the defendant’s business where a more limited restraint could adequately protect the applicant.

The relief should therefore distinguish between the allegedly unlawful activity and the defendant’s legitimate commercial operations. For example, where the dispute concerns particular confidential data, the proposed order should not unnecessarily restrain the use of information already in the public domain or independently developed by the defendant.

The applicant should also consider whether an undertaking, deposit, disclosure order, maintenance of accounts or preservation of particular property could provide sufficient protection without requiring a complete prohibition.

## Irreparable Injury

The applicant must ordinarily establish that refusal of interim relief would cause an injury that cannot be adequately remedied by an award of damages or another final order.

The expression “irreparable injury” does not necessarily mean that the harm is physically incapable of correction. It generally refers to harm that cannot be sufficiently quantified or compensated in monetary terms.

Potential examples may include:

* destruction or transfer of unique property;
* disclosure of confidential information;
* continuing infringement of proprietary rights;
* loss of control over a distinctive business asset;
* diversion of an opportunity that cannot later be reconstructed;
* dissipation of assets intended to defeat a decree; or
* irreversible alteration of the subject matter of the suit.

A purely financial loss is not automatically incapable of supporting an injunction. The court may consider whether damages can be realistically calculated and recovered. However, where the dispute is essentially a claim for payment by a financially capable defendant, the availability of damages may weigh against injunctive relief.

The applicant should avoid making general assertions that its reputation, goodwill or business will suffer. The pleadings should explain the commercial mechanism through which the harm will occur and why a later award would not provide an adequate remedy.

## Ex Parte Injunctions

An ex parte injunction is granted without first hearing the affected defendant. Because such an order interferes with the defendant’s rights before it has an opportunity to respond, ex parte relief is reserved for situations requiring immediate protection.

Order XXXIX Rule 3 of the Code of Civil Procedure ordinarily requires notice of an injunction application to be given to the opposite party. The court may proceed without notice where delay would defeat the object of granting the injunction, but it must record the reasons for doing so. The applicant must then comply with the prescribed requirements concerning delivery of the application, affidavit, plaint and supporting documents to the opposite party.

An applicant seeking ex parte relief must make full and fair disclosure of all material facts, including facts that may assist the defendant. This obligation is particularly important because the court is initially dependent upon the applicant’s presentation of the dispute.

The application should disclose:

* previous correspondence and negotiations;
* contractual provisions relied upon by the defendant;
* earlier proceedings or orders;
* any delay by the applicant;
* material defences already communicated;
* relevant settlements or admissions; and
* facts that may affect jurisdiction or maintainability.

Suppression or selective presentation of material facts may result in the injunction being vacated and may affect the applicant’s credibility throughout the litigation.

The proposed ex parte order should be precise and capable of compliance. Vague restraints against interfering with the applicant’s “business interests” or acting “illegally” may create uncertainty and enforcement difficulties.

## Preservation of Assets and Evidence

In some commercial disputes, the principal concern is not continuing contractual conduct but the risk that assets, documents or evidence will be transferred, destroyed or concealed before judgment.

The Code of Civil Procedure provides separate mechanisms that may be relevant, including temporary injunctions, attachment before judgment, appointment of a receiver, inspection, production and preservation of property.

Attachment before judgment is not intended to provide an unsecured claimant with premature security merely because a monetary claim has been filed. The applicant must satisfy the statutory conditions, including showing conduct indicating an intention to obstruct or delay execution of a possible decree.

Similarly, an order restraining the disposal of assets should not ordinarily be sought on the basis of an unsupported apprehension that the defendant may become unable to pay. The application should identify the property, the relevant transactions and the conduct demonstrating a genuine risk to enforcement.

Where preservation of documents or electronic records is required, the relief should specify the categories of material to be retained. Overbroad orders covering every document or device connected with the defendant’s business may be disproportionate and difficult to supervise.

The applicant should preserve its own evidence before approaching the court. Relevant emails, messages, contracts, invoices, system records and publicly available materials should be collected in a manner that maintains their completeness and authenticity.

## Contractual and Statutory Restrictions

The Specific Relief Act, 1963 and the Code of Civil Procedure govern the availability and form of injunctive relief. An injunction remains discretionary even where a legal right and breach are shown. Temporary injunctions are regulated by the Code, while permanent injunctions may be granted through a decree after adjudication.

The court must also consider statutory restrictions on injunctions. Relief may be refused where the applicant has acquiesced in the continuing breach, where an equally effective remedy is available through another usual mode of proceeding or where the applicant’s own conduct disentitles it to equitable relief.

In contractual matters, the court should examine whether the obligation is capable of specific enforcement. A negative covenant may sometimes be restrained even where the court would not compel affirmative performance of every obligation under the agreement. The result depends upon the terms of the contract, the nature of the relationship and the statutory restrictions applicable to specific performance and injunctions.

Courts are generally cautious about orders that require continuous judicial supervision of complex commercial activity. The applicant should therefore frame relief in a manner that establishes a clear standard of compliance.

An injunction cannot ordinarily be used to rewrite the parties’ bargain, create a commercial arrangement they did not agree upon or provide the applicant with substantially the final relief without the safeguards of trial.

## Enforcement and Variation of Interim Orders

An interim order should be communicated promptly and accurately to the persons required to comply with it. The operative portion, date, scope and duration of the order should be carefully reviewed before alleging non-compliance.

Order XXXIX Rule 2A of the Code of Civil Procedure provides consequences for disobedience or breach of an injunction granted under Rules 1 or 2. The court may order attachment of the property of the person guilty of disobedience and may also order detention in civil prison, subject to the statutory framework.

Before initiating enforcement proceedings, the applicant should establish:

* knowledge or service of the order;
* the precise obligation imposed;
* the act or omission constituting breach;
* the identity of the person responsible; and
* supporting documentary or electronic evidence.

Not every disagreement regarding interpretation amounts to deliberate disobedience. An order that is unclear or reasonably capable of more than one interpretation may be difficult to enforce through coercive proceedings.

Either party may seek variation, clarification or vacation of an interim injunction where circumstances change, material facts emerge or the order causes consequences not previously considered.

A defendant opposing continuation of the injunction should place its substantive defence and evidence before the court rather than relying only upon technical objections to the initial order.

## Appeals and Appellate Restraint

Orders granting or refusing temporary injunctions may be appealable under the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure read with the Commercial Courts Act, where applicable.

Section 13 of the Commercial Courts Act provides the appellate framework for judgments and orders of Commercial Courts and Commercial Divisions. Appeals under the provision are subject to the categories and restrictions recognised by the Act and the Code, and the statutory period is ordinarily sixty days.

An appellate court does not ordinarily substitute its discretion merely because it might have reached a different conclusion at first instance. Interference is generally justified where the lower court exercised discretion arbitrarily, capriciously or perversely, disregarded settled principles or relied upon irrelevant considerations.

The appellant should therefore identify a legal or evaluative error in the injunction order rather than simply rearguing the application. Relevant grounds may include:

* failure to apply the three established requirements;
* material misreading of the contract or documents;
* reliance upon unsupported findings;
* refusal to consider relevant evidence;
* disproportionate or unworkable relief;
* absence of reasons for ex parte intervention; or
* grant of relief extending beyond the suit or application.

Where an injunction has already altered the commercial position, the appellate court may also consider the consequences of disturbing the arrangement pending final adjudication.

## Conclusion

Interim injunctions are an important means of protecting commercial rights where final relief may otherwise become ineffective. They are not, however, granted merely because a breach is alleged or because the dispute involves substantial commercial value.

The applicant must demonstrate a credible legal right, a genuine need for immediate protection, comparative hardship in its favour and harm that cannot adequately be addressed through damages. The supporting pleadings and documents should establish urgency rather than merely asserting it.

A successful strategy also requires proportionate drafting. The relief should preserve the subject matter of the dispute without unnecessarily restraining legitimate business activity or effectively deciding the suit before trial.

**The effectiveness of an interim injunction application depends on genuine urgency, complete disclosure, strong contemporaneous evidence and relief carefully tailored to the threatened harm.**
